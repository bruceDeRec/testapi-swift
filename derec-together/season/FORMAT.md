# Many Hands: series format

*The format for the DeRec Alliance YouTube series, hosted by Bruce Longley and Facundo La Rocca. Every choice here comes with its reason. **[HOST GATE]** marks something that needs Bruce's or Facundo's sign-off. **[VERIFY]** marks a fact to check before it goes on camera. The full list of both is at the end.*

---

## The format on one page

- **Name:** ***Many Hands***, a DeRec Alliance community podcast, as ruled in DECISIONS R2. Fallback, if the hosts want "DeRec" in the title: *The DeRec Table*. [HOST GATE]
- **Flagship:** *Many Hands*, 12–18 minutes (15:00 as standard), every other Wednesday at 11:00 New York time, published as a Premiere.
- **Weekly beat:** *Light Work*, a vertical Short of 30–60 seconds, every Friday.
- **The spine:** say the two tier names together and you get the proverb *many hands make light work*. The hosts split it to sign off. Bruce: "Many hands—" Facundo: "—light work."
- **Run of show:** Bumper, The Question, The Short Answer, The Table, a feature (Hands On or Heard It Somewhere, alternating), Where It Stops, Raised Hands, Say It Back, One Small Thing, Next Time, End Card.
- **The limit is stated straight after the episode's most impressive moment,** and never at the end, where it would sound like a disclaimer.
- **No music under talk. Ever.**
- **Mascot:** **Tuck**, a grown sea otter, with her friend **Pip**, a mug-sized porcelain café robot (R1). Tuck is always in the bumper and the end card, and may appear as a small still ornament on four segment tabs. Pip joins only the ensemble activities and is never in the ritual. Neither appears on screen while a limit or risk is being stated, in a demo or on a thumbnail. Neither talks, and neither is ever called a helper. Pip never plays one; Tuck plays one only in the wordless ritual (§7).
- **The loop:** every episode asks the audience one specific question. The answers feed the next Light Work and the next Raised Hands. What the hosts can't answer goes on the IOU card, and they pay it back on camera.

## 1. The name

### What the name has to do

In order of importance:

1. **Sound right when Bruce says it** at 0:17 of every episode for years, without wearing thin.
2. **Pass the Grandma test** the first time she hears it.
3. **Sit on the cover the client likes** without breaking it.
4. **Be easy to find again** for someone who heard it once.

It also has one veto: **it must not promise safety or teach the wrong idea of how DeRec works.** The title is the first thing the show teaches.

**Searchability, realistically.** Nobody searches for a show they haven't heard of. New viewers find a small educational channel through episode titles (the questions people actually type, like "what happens if I lose my phone") and through suggested videos. So the show name's search job is narrower: someone who watched once can find it again. That means the name has to be unambiguous next to the channel name "DeRec Alliance". It doesn't have to be unique worldwide. "DeRec" is a rare word. It is shared with a few unrelated names (the OECD's DEReC database and a Swiss dental app, derec®), and paired with "Alliance" it finds only the Alliance. [VERIFY on YouTube search before launch.] It already appears on every video through the channel name and the title suffix. That leaves the show name free to be a real English phrase.

### Why *Many Hands* wins

- **It's the protocol in a proverb.** No single hand holds the secret, and a single share reveals nothing. It takes enough hands together. Grandma understands that before anyone says "Shamir".
- **It also describes the Alliance and the community.** The Alliance is twenty-plus organisations with none of them in charge. The community is the contributors call, the comments, and the people who send in questions.
- **It matches the picture.** The most useful reference image shows Bruce and Facundo placing amber gems on a café table: secret-sharing done with hands. The show's name, its hands-on demo segment and its best image all express one idea.
- **It's honest.** Nothing in it promises safety.
- **Its second half gives the format a spine.** The flagship is *Many Hands* and the weekly Short is *Light Work*. The hosts split the proverb to sign off, so neither of them says the whole thing. (That's a playful nod, not a description of the maths.)
- **It's classic and modern at once.** The proverb is older than anyone watching, and the lockup brings the modern light. That's the client's "classy and classic, with a touch of modernity" in two words.

**The honest cost:** "Many Hands" on its own isn't unique. These four fixes are required, not optional:

1. Every video title ends "`| Many Hands NN`" and sits under the channel name DeRec Alliance.
2. The YouTube podcast playlist is named "**Many Hands — the DeRec Alliance community podcast**".
3. Every description opens with "*Many Hands* is the DeRec Alliance community podcast…".
4. The imprint "DeRec Alliance" is on screen in the bumper's final frame and on the end card.

## 2. Audience, promise and content pillars

### Who it's for

**Primary: everyday owners.** People with digital things they can't replace: family photos, passwords, a patient portal, perhaps a wallet someone set up for them. They have never heard of DeRec. Often they're the person in the family who "does the computer stuff", but they aren't security people. The protocol's mission is recovery for the mass market, and these people are the mass market. They arrive from search with a worried or curious question.

**Secondary: builders.** Wallet and app developers, staff at member organisations, and contributors weighing DeRec up. They already have the GitBook docs and the contributors call, so the show isn't their manual. They come for Hands On and for Facundo's precision, and to find something they can send to their own users. They must never be bored, but no sentence is written for them alone.

**The bar, not the target: a 70-year-old who has never heard the word "blockchain".** Every episode is checked against her. She is the reason for three rules:

- **No music under talk.** Age-related hearing loss makes speech over music hard to follow.
- **Human-corrected captions on every upload.** Auto-captions garble "DeRec" and "Shamir".
- **The Jargon Jar sits on the table** (see §6).

### The promise

This goes in the channel description and the trailer, and is spoken in the pilot:

> **Every other week, two friends take one real question about losing, and getting back, the digital things that matter, and answer it in words anyone can follow, including where recovery stops.**

The short form for the banner and social bios: *"One real question. Two friends. Plain answers about getting back what matters, limits included."*

Each episode gets checked against three words in the promise: ***one*** question (not three), ***anyone*** (Grandma), ***stops*** (the limit gets said).

### Three content pillars

1. **What if…** These are the moments something gets lost: a phone in a lake, a dead laptop, a forgotten password, a parent who kept everything in their head. Titles start from the situation. This pillar opens every season and brings in search traffic, because these are the questions people type.
2. **How it works.** The mechanism, shown on the table: owners, helpers, shares, the threshold, the daily check-in, re-sharing, and what a helper does and doesn't know. It also covers how the open project is built: the Rust library, the spec, how the code gets made. Hands On lives here.
3. **Who you trust.** People: choosing helpers, being a helper, the family conversation, and what the Alliance is (an open standard from many organisations, with nobody in charge). It also covers the community building it, and the everyday habits that recovery doesn't replace.

**Rule for the season plan:** any three consecutive flagships cover all three pillars, and two *How it works* episodes never run back to back. Grandma drifts off during the mechanism, and people and situations bring her back.

## 3. The publishing ladder

### The two tiers

| | ***Many Hands*** (flagship) | ***Light Work*** (weekly beat) |
|---|---|---|
| **Format** | 16:9, 12–18 minutes. 15:00 is standard; 10- and 20-minute versions are for exceptions (§4). | 9:16 Short, 30–60 seconds, with burned-in captions |
| **Cadence** | Every other Wednesday, 11:00 New York time | Every Friday, 11:00 New York time |
| **Published as** | A Premiere, with Bruce in the live chat | A standard upload |
| **What it contains** | One question, with the full run of show | **In flagship weeks:** the best 45–60 seconds of that episode. **In call weeks:** one community question answered in under a minute, recorded during the flagship session |
| **Linked to** | The *Many Hands* podcast playlist; the audio also goes to Spotify and Apple Podcasts | Always the newest flagship, through YouTube's related-video link |
| **Its job** | Build the library: evergreen answers that keep finding viewers through search for years | Keep the channel visibly alive between flagships, reach people who have never heard of it, and test which questions draw viewers |

The Short is the "light work": small, quick and easy. Said together, the two tier names make the proverb. [HOST GATE]

**Light Work has three rules of its own.** The question is on screen and spoken in the first second. Tuck appears only as the one-second stamp at the loop point (§7). And a clip that shows off what DeRec can do must include its limit line, or it doesn't run. The limit line is never the last thing said before the stamp.

**A season is 12 flagships, or 24 weeks.** Between seasons, the contributors call carries on and so does Light Work, cut from the back catalogue, so the channel never goes quiet.

### The two-week pulse

The DeRec contributors call has met every two weeks since 21 October 2024, about fifty cycles so far. [VERIFY how many calls were held and the current day and time.] The show adopts a rhythm the team already keeps instead of inventing one it has to learn.

The show alternates with the call rather than landing in the same week. 21 October 2024 was a Monday, so this grid assumes the call is still on Mondays. If it has moved, shift the grid but keep the alternation. [VERIFY]

| Week | Monday | Tuesday | Wednesday | Friday |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A: call week** | Contributors call. Flagship N+1 locks at 17:00. | Record flagship N+2 and one standalone Light Work | – | **Light Work:** answers a community question from episode N's comments and teases N+1 |
| **B: show week** | – | – | ***Many Hands* N+1 premieres** | **Light Work:** a clip from N+1 |

Why it's arranged this way:

- **The Alliance says something public every week:** the call one week, the show the next.
- **Recording the day after the call** fits a slot the hosts already keep, and questions raised on the call are a day old rather than weeks old.
- **Three weeks between recording and premiere**, with the lock nine days before the Premiere, gives a small team time to edit, caption and fact-check calmly. Recording two ahead keeps one finished episode in reserve at all times (PLAYBOOK §9). [HOST GATE]
- **Launch with two episodes in the can** (R8), so one sick week never breaks the chain. Episode 02 records off the grid once, on Tuesday 27 October, to make that true.

**The one exception in season one: Christmas week.** Friday 25 December would carry Episode 04's clip. Instead it carries **LW007, a banked standalone** recorded on 15 December: *New phone today? Check this before you wipe the old one.* It's a general habit with no DeRec claim, so it needs no limit line. **LW008, Episode 04's clip**, moves to Friday 1 January, in place of that call week's community answer. Episode 04's pinned thread feeds LW010 on 15 January instead. Both holiday Shorts are scheduled in advance, because nobody works either day. The full holiday table is in `season/SEASON-ONE.md`. [HOST GATE]

### Why this ladder suits how YouTube treats a small educational channel

- **Each long-form video is judged on its own, by whether viewers are satisfied.** YouTube's discovery team has said repeatedly that the system looks at how each video does with the people it's shown to, and doesn't reward how often a channel uploads. That means whether people click, how long they stay and whether they come back. [VERIFY: Creator Insider, "YouTube Algorithm 2026", 1 September 2026.] So a fortnightly episode that holds its audience beats a weekly one that doesn't. Both hosts have day jobs, and Facundo is building the library the show talks about. Every two weeks is the pace at which both can arrive prepared every time.
- **Small educational channels grow through search and suggested videos, on evergreen questions.** An answer to "what happens to my passwords if I lose my phone?" can find viewers for years. That's why:
  - flagship titles are questions people type;
  - chapters are written in plain language, since they can appear as key moments in search;
  - the show stays away from news.
- **Shorts are recommended by a separate system, to a separate audience.** YouTube's creator liaison has said Shorts don't hurt long-form videos. [VERIFY] Shorts reach people however many subscribers a channel has, which a new channel badly needs. But Shorts viewers rarely go on to become long-form viewers by themselves. So Light Work never pretends to be the show: every Short links to the newest flagship through YouTube's related-video feature, and success is measured on the flagship.
- **A Short that takes off is a topic signal.** If a Light Work answer beats the others, that question becomes a flagship. It's cheap audience research.
- **Two weeks of silence is a long time for a new channel.** Subscribers don't see or click most uploads in their feeds [VERIFY], so a channel that appears once a fortnight is easy to forget. The Friday beat keeps it in view.
- **Premieres earn their keep through the live chat.** A Premiere gives the episode a watch page with a countdown and a live chat. Bruce is the Alliance's community lead, and his presence in that chat is the first step of the question loop (§8). Facundo joins when he can.
- **Ten to twenty minutes suits a first-time viewer.** A cold viewer will commit to fifteen minutes far more readily than to forty-five, so the client was right to shorten it.
- **Podcast playlist.** Designate the flagship playlist as a podcast in YouTube Studio, so episodes also appear as a podcast on YouTube Music, and publish the audio to Spotify and Apple Podcasts. That brings one format rule with it: **anything shown on screen must also be said out loud** (see Hands On).
- **Disclosure.** The ritual master is animated by hand, and the music is written by a commissioned human composer (R9). [HOST GATE] The only AI left on the table is assistance on bar one's background plates, and only if the hosts choose it. [HOST GATE] Any AI element that ships is disclosed with YouTube's altered or synthetic content label and a plain line in every description. The conversation and the Shorts are always real footage of the real hosts. There are no AI likenesses and no AI voices anywhere inside an episode.

**Chapters.** The description lists a chapter for every segment. Each title is the segment name plus plain-language content, for example "7:10 Hands On: getting Bruce's recipe back with a ruler". YouTube requires the first chapter at 0:00, at least three chapters, and at least 10 seconds per chapter. The Bumper and The Question share the 0:00 chapter to clear that last rule.

**Segment tabs.** Each segment opens with a two-second tab in the show's typeface, set at the top of the frame like a book's running head, clear of the caption band (brand kit), and Bruce says the segment's name aloud, both as ritual and so audio listeners know where they are. Every tab except Where It Stops gets a musical sting of a second or less, taken from the theme. Where It Stops gets silence. Inside an episode there are no full-screen chapter cards, because they feel like ad breaks.

**Length rule.** The question sets the length, not the edit:

- A narrow, yes-or-no question ("Do my helpers know each other?") gets the 10-minute version.
- A how or why question gets the 15.
- A double feature or a clinic gets the 20.

Never pad an episode. If a 15 comes in at 12:40 because the answer was clean, publish it at 12:40.

## 5. The first 60 seconds after the bumper (0:05–1:05)

The music hands over to people. It doesn't hand over to silence, and it doesn't hand over to more music.

- **The first picture after the bumper is the two of them together.** The partnership is the product, so the viewer's first sight of the humans must include both.
- **The title's question, in the title's words, within ten seconds.**
- **The names come after the question and before the promise.**

### The shape

| Time | Beat | What happens | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **0:05–0:07** | **Landing** (2s) | Cut to the wide two-shot on the bumper's final downbeat. The last chord rings for about 1.5 seconds under the picture and has died away before Bruce's second sentence. ## 6. Recurring segments

There are seven recurring pieces. Four appear in every episode, two alternate as the feature, and one is always on the table.

| Segment | When it appears |
|---|---|
| **Where It Stops** | Every episode |
| **Raised Hands** | Every episode |
| **Say It Back** | Every episode |
| **One Small Thing** | Every episode |
| **Hands On** | Odd-numbered episodes (the feature) |
| **Heard It Somewhere** | Even-numbered episodes (the feature) |
| **The Jargon Jar** | Always on the table |

**The features alternate strictly.** Viewers learn the rhythm, and Facundo has to prepare a demo every four weeks rather than every two. Episode 1 is odd, so the show opens with something done by hand. The 20-minute version runs both features.

### Hands On: the rehearsal

Facundo shows it working, either with his hands on the table or with software on screen.

1. **Harmless data only.** Every demo uses the same secret: Bruce's chili recipe (§8). A real key, seed phrase, password or helper identity never appears on screen.
2. **Two kinds of demo, labelled on screen and never mixed up:**
   - A **tabletop model** uses physical tokens on the table: amber glass, echoing the café image. It's a metaphor, and Facundo says what it gets wrong. When the maths needs a picture, it's **the Curve** (R6): an ink line on paper that wobbles through every shape while it has fewer points than the threshold, and snaps to one shape at the threshold. Facundo states its limits with it: the secret is one number, not the whole curve; real shares live in modular arithmetic, so there's no smooth line to look at; the smooth curve is the classroom picture. The gold light thread never appears in teaching.
   - **Real software** means the `lib-derec` library or an app built on it, with the version shown on screen. Today that's "v0.0.1-alpha.3" [VERIFY at recording].
3. **Say what you see.** Every action is narrated so podcast listeners can follow: "I'm handing out four shares. Bruce, take one."
4. **End with two sentences:** what the demo just showed, and what it didn't prove. A successful rehearsal shows the steps work with harmless data. It doesn't prove that your own setup is right.
5. **If it fails live, keep it in.** Facundo explains what happened. On a trust show, a failure handled calmly is worth more than a perfect take.
6. **3:30 at most.** No sped-up screen recordings. If something takes a while, cut it and say that you cut it.

### Heard It Somewhere: the myth-buster

Bruce reads out something people say, and Facundo gives a verdict.

1. **Every myth is real.** It comes from the comments, a forum, the call or a relative, and is credited in general terms ("from our comments", "a common one online"). Never name or shame a person. Never use a myth about a member organisation or a competing product.
2. **Only three verdicts, stamped on screen:** **True.** **Not true.** **True, but…** The third is the honest one, and it'll be the most common.
3. **The verdict comes first**, in Facundo's first sentence. The reason follows, in three sentences or fewer.
4. **Two myths per segment in a 15-minute episode, one in a 10.**
5. **If the "but" is a limit, say it the way Where It Stops is said:** plainly, with no music and no mascot.
6. **Nobody gets laughed at.** Believing a myth is normal; the segment's name says so.

Three that the brief's facts support (confirm the wording with Facundo [VERIFY]):

- *"Each helper holds a piece of my password."* → **Not true.** A single share reveals nothing about the secret, not even one letter.
- *"This is only for crypto people."* → **Not true.** The secret can be a wallet key, a password or any other secret.
- *"My helpers could team up and take it."* → **True, but…** Enough of them together (the threshold) could restore it, and fewer than that learn nothing, even working together. Helpers also aren't told who the other helpers are or how many there are. So who you choose matters.

### Where It Stops: stating the limit with confidence

Bruce always opens it with **"So where does it stop?"** Facundo always ends his answer with **"…and that's where it stops."** Bruce may add one line of reaction after that.

Here's what makes it sound like confidence rather than a disclaimer:

1. **Placement.** It comes straight after the episode's most impressive moment, usually the end of Hands On, so the limit arrives attached to the claim it limits. It never runs in the last two minutes, which is where disclaimers go.
2. **The builder says it.** Facundo published the library's first Rust crates, and an engineer stating the limits of work he helps build is the most confident voice in the show.
3. **Words about scope, not apology.**
   - Say: "Its job is…", "It doesn't…", "That's a different tool:", "That's still your job."
   - Never say: "unfortunately", "we can't guarantee", "please note", "of course, nothing is a hundred percent".
4. **Always point to what does handle it.** Each limit ends with the thing that covers it: your bank's fraud line, your phone's passcode, two-factor sign-in on your email. Name general habits only, never a product.
5. **The room goes still.** No music, no mascot, no B-roll, no cutaways, and the two-shot holds. One text band carries the key line in the show's ink colour on paper. No red, no warning triangles, no padlocks.
6. **The same ritual lines every time.** When a limit is always said the same way, it stops sounding like a caveat and starts sounding like a signature. A pilot's announcements sound calm for the same reason: they're always the same.
7. **30–50 seconds, in every episode, with no exceptions.**

The hosts' own standards set four limits (non-negotiable 1). Recovery doesn't undo a hack. It doesn't reverse a transaction you authorised. It doesn't stop a scam. It doesn't replace looking after your devices and accounts. Each episode states the one or two that apply to its question, in that episode's terms, rather than reciting the whole list.

> **BRUCE:** So where does it stop?
> **FACUNDO:** Its job is getting your secret back to you. If someone already got in and moved things, recovery doesn't undo that. If you approved a payment to a scammer, it doesn't reverse it. And it's not the lock on your phone. That's still your passcode. It gets your secret back, and that's where it stops.
> **BRUCE:** So the front door is still my job.
> **FACUNDO:** Still your job.

### Raised Hands: the community question

Bruce says "Raised hands," then reads the question out word for word.

1. **Real questions only**, credited by first name or handle. They come from:
   - the pinned question under the last episode, and other comments;
   - the contributors call (credited "from the contributors call");
   - the Alliance's community channels [VERIFY which ones].

   The team never writes its own softballs.
2. **Consent.** The pinned comment says questions may be read on air by first name [HOST GATE on the wording]. Never read out financial details, amounts, or anything that reveals someone's setup. Paraphrase instead, and say that you did.
3. **If the hosts owe an answer from the IOU card (§8), they pay it first**, in 30 seconds.
4. **If they can't answer fully, they say so** and the question goes on the IOU card. They never bluff.
5. **"Which coin? Which wallet? Which company?"** gets the house answer, said warmly: **"We don't pick winners here."** Then they answer the real question underneath: what to look for, not whom to pick. That's the neutral-education rule said out loud, and it will become a line the show is known for.
6. **One question in a 10- or 15-minute episode, two in a 20.**
7. **The asker's name appears on screen and in the description.**

### Say It Back: the teach-back

1. **Bruce says it, not Facundo.** If the audience's stand-in can say it, that's the proof Grandma can too.
2. **One sentence, and no jargon.** The Jar is watching.
3. **Facundo scores it with Percent Right (§8).** He may fix one word, for accuracy only, never for polish.
4. **20–30 seconds.** If Bruce gets it badly wrong, that's good television: Facundo fixes it, they laugh, and Bruce says it again.

### One Small Thing: the take-home

1. **Free, under ten minutes, and doable tonight by anyone, with or without DeRec.**
2. **Never "download", "buy" or "sign up", and never a product or member name.** Stick to a neutral habit, such as:
   - check that your phone is backing up;
   - write down who you'd ask to be a helper, and why;
   - ask a parent where their passwords live.
3. **Bruce says it.** It appears on a clean card, the frame people screenshot, and it's the first line of the description.
4. **It ties back to the episode's question.** It's never a generic security tip.

### The Jargon Jar: always on the table

A glass jar sits on Bruce's side of the table, with amber tokens for Facundo and clear ones for Bruce. When either host uses a word Grandma wouldn't know without explaining it, the other drops one of the offender's tokens in.

1. **It's for unexplained words, not technical words.** "Threshold" is fine if you say what it means. Say it without explaining and it costs a token.
2. **It applies to both hosts.** "Web3" from Bruce counts.
3. **One appeal, in one sentence.** Bruce rules on Facundo's appeals, and Facundo rules on Bruce's.
4. **Viewers can call it.** If the hosts missed one and a viewer posts a timestamped "Jargon Jar!" comment, a token goes in next episode, with credit.
5. **The running tally appears on the end card.** At the end of the season, whoever has more tokens in the jar buys the coffee, on camera, at the finale clinic.
6. **Facundo has to enjoy this.** [HOST GATE] It affectionately teases his precision. If it ever looks like mockery, retire it.

Why the Jar is on the table:

- It enforces the Grandma test on camera.
- It makes Facundo's precision lovable instead of intimidating.
- It gives viewers a way to join in before they're ready to ask a question.

## 7. The mascot's place in the format

The mascot is **Tuck**, a grown sea otter, with **Pip**, a mug-sized porcelain café robot, as her friend (DECISIONS R1). Their design, movement and sound, the locked ritual and Tuck's Year of activities are in `mascot/CHARACTER-BIBLE.md`. Every frame number is in `intro/COLD-OPEN-STORYBOARD.md`. This section sets only where they may appear in the format.

"The mascot" below means Tuck. Every "never" applies to Pip as well, and Pip appears in fewer places still: bar one of the ensemble activities (Episodes 04, 07, 10 and 12 in season one), those episodes' end-card reprises, and channel furniture. Pip is never in the ritual and **never holds a stone** (R1).

**The principle: the mascot sets the mood. It never explains, never reassures and never sells.** It belongs to the show's rituals, not to its teaching.

### Always

- **The Bumper (0:00–0:05), in every episode.** Bar one: one small, delightful activity, different each time and matched to the month it airs. Bar two: the ritual, identical every time and locked forever (R3). This is Tuck's only full animated performance in a flagship.
- **The End Card (the last 20 seconds), in every episode.** A small reprise of the same activity, now resolved. In Episode 01 she dozes afloat, the maple leaf slid down over her eyes like a sun hat. The bookend rewards viewers who stayed to the end.
  - It sits in the brand kit's reprise area, clear of YouTube's end-screen elements: a held frame with one small looping motion. It never shows a stone or a thread.
  - **Sound:** under the reprise, the outtake carries laughter and room tone only. No music under talk. The reprise's own sound is the resolved twin of the activity sound, placed in a gap in the outtake, or left out.
  - Each episode's reprise is in the bible's production companion.

### Allowed

- **Segment tabs for the four lighter segments:** Heard It Somewhere, Raised Hands, Say It Back and One Small Thing. It appears as a small, still ornament, like a printer's flourish in a well-set book, not as an animation: Tuck in her home float pose as a one-colour Brass line engraving. Tuck only, never Pip. It's gone when the tab goes, after two seconds.
- **The One Small Thing card.** Tuck may point at the action, with both paws together. This is the one place she may be encouraging, because the card is an invitation, not a claim.
- **Light Work Shorts: a one-second stamp at the very end**, where the Short loops. It is cut from the ritual master, f88–f111: three dull stones, all three light, the threads begin to rise (storyboard §7). Tuck only, never Pip. Never in the first second. In the Shorts feed, the first second decides whether people swipe away, and the hook is the question.
- **Channel furniture:** the banner, community posts, stickers and merchandise. Pip may join Tuck here. The Alliance channel keeps its own avatar. Nothing is sold until season one has aired and the bible's clearance steps are done. [HOST GATE]

### Never

1. **Never on screen while a limit, risk or failure is being stated.** That covers:
   - Where It Stops;
   - a "True, but…" verdict whose "but" is a limit;
   - a Raised Hands answer that becomes a limit;
   - a live demo that fails.

   A smiling character beside "this won't reverse a scam" trivialises the sentence and looks like a friendly face selling the limit away. A sad one is worse. The rule is mechanical so the editor never has to judge it: **whenever anyone is saying that recovery doesn't, can't or won't do something, the mascot is off screen.**
2. **Never takes a part in explaining the protocol.** It doesn't play a helper, a share or the secret, and it doesn't "guard" anything or carry tokens in a diagram. A cute character doing the protocol's work turns a metaphor into a claim, and it teaches the wrong idea: in DeRec, helpers are people and organisations you choose.
   - **The one exception is the ritual in bar two** (R3): Tuck's stone, two friends' stones, and all three lighting at once. It is wordless and never labelled. The three stones meet at the lens because the viewer is the owner: real shares go back only to the owner's new phone, and helpers never meet or pool them. The hosts mention it once, in Episode 01, where Bruce says R7's line: *"Tuck's a cartoon. Your helpers are real people you choose."*
   - **No spec word (owner, helper, share, secret) is ever a name or an on-screen label** (R7). Tuck is Tuck, and Pip is Pip.
   - The client's "DeRec helper" idea is answered by Pip, who is Tuck's friend and never anyone's helper. Pip never holds a stone, because a robot holding one would teach "an AI keeps my keys" (R1).
3. **Never inside a Hands On demo or a screen recording.** There it would look like part of the software.
4. **Never in the first 60 seconds after the bumper.** The handoff to the hosts stays clean.
5. **Never on flagship thumbnails.** Thumbnails show the two hosts and the question. Their faces are the product, and a cartoon on a thumbnail tells a cold viewer the show is for children.
6. **Never next to a member organisation's name or logo, a price, money or a chart.** Nothing that implies endorsement and no financial imagery.
7. **Never speaks.** No voice, no words, no speech bubbles: music and sound effects only. A voice decides too much about the character, and a synthetic voice would sit badly on a trust show.
8. **Never interrupts the conversation.** Nothing pops up during The Short Answer, The Table or Raised Hands. While the hosts are talking, the hosts are the show.

**One rule for the mascot team:** the bumper's activity is never announced or previewed before the episode. The reveal belongs to the first five seconds, and it's the reason regulars watch the bumper instead of skipping it. There is no exception. The storyboard's 15-second four-activity cut (storyboard §7) runs only from Wednesday 10 February 2027, as the head of TR03 (`production/CHANNEL-KIT.md` §1), after Episodes 01, 02, 04 and 07 have aired. The launch trailer, TR01, shows only The Float. [HOST GATE]

## 8. The loop

This is how each episode hands the next one its material.

### The chain

1. **Next Time asks the audience one specific question**, pinned as the first comment. It's never "let us know in the comments". It's a question with an answer: *"Who's the first person you'd ask to be a helper, and why? First names only."*
2. **Bruce answers in the Premiere chat** and carries the best replies into the call-week recording.
3. **The call-week Light Work, on Friday, answers one of them** in under a minute, credits the asker, and teases the next flagship.
4. **The flagship recorded that call week answers another in Raised Hands.** Because the show records two ahead, that's the episode two after the one that asked (`season/SEASON-ONE.md`). The person in The Question often comes from the same thread, so the viewer who asked hears their name at 0:25.
5. **Whatever the hosts can't answer goes on the IOU card.**

**The IOU card** is a physical card propped in the middle of the table, always visible in the wide shot. It lists the answers the show owes: things the hosts didn't know, and claims marked [VERIFY] that Facundo wants to check. Each Raised Hands pays off one debt first, when one is owed. The card makes the show's honesty visible: *"we said we'd check, and we checked."*

**The contributors call is part of the loop.** Questions from the call feed the show ("this came up on Monday's contributors call"), and the show points builders to the call as the next step deeper. [VERIFY that the call is open to the public, and how to join, before saying so on air.]

**Viewers suggest Tuck's activities.** The description invites viewers to suggest what Tuck does next. Next Time doesn't, because it makes one request only. The best suggestion becomes a future bumper, credited in the description. It's the easiest way into the community for someone who doesn't have a question yet. A suggestion can replace any activity from Episode 05 on, if it passes the bible's activity rules and matches the month it airs. Activities are locked two episodes ahead so production has time. [HOST GATE]

### Running jokes

There are three, and none of them is forced. If one doesn't come up naturally in an episode, it doesn't appear.

1. **The Recipe.** Every Hands On demo splits the same secret: Bruce's chili recipe. Bruce insists it's the most valuable secret on the show, and Facundo insists it needs salt. [HOST GATE: Bruce picks the dish, and it should be one he really makes.] When the recipe is restored on screen, Bruce's hand always covers the secret ingredient: *"That part's between me and my helpers."* The ingredient is finally revealed at the season-finale clinic. Why a recipe:
   - it's harmless data;
   - it's the original family secret, handed down through generations;
   - it makes the cover's point that most of what's worth protecting isn't money.
2. **Percent Right.** Bruce reaches for analogies and Facundo rates them. *"It's like giving each neighbour a piece of the spare key." "Forty percent. Half a key still gives something away. A share on its own gives away nothing."* [VERIFY the wording with Facundo.] In one exchange you get the spark and the steady hand. It also teaches the audience something most explainers hide: every analogy is partly wrong, and a good explainer tells you which part. Percent Right is also how Say It Back gets scored.
3. **The Jargon Jar tally**, kept across the season and settled with coffee at the finale.

### Lines the show will be known for

These are said the same way every time, so they become the show's voice:

- *"…and this is Many Hands."* (the opening)
- *"So where does it stop?"* / *"…and that's where it stops."*
- *"Still your job."*
- *"We don't pick winners here."*
- *"Many hands—"* / *"—light work."* (the sign-off)

### The season payoff

The last flagship of each season is **the clinic**, in the 20-minute version:

- Raised Hands takes over with community questions only.
- The IOU card is cleared.
- The Jargon Jar is counted and the loser buys the coffee.
- The secret ingredient is revealed.

Everything the loop has built up pays off in one episode. That's what makes someone who has watched eleven episodes want to watch the twelfth.
