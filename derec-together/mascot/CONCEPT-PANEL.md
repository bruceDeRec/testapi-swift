## What each judge said

### Charm and repeatability: winner Tuck

Through a regular viewer's eyes, the question is which bumper still makes you smile at week 60, not which is cleverest at week one. Tuck wins because her repeating part carries a variable inside it: the carry-over detail makes you study the locked shot every single week. That is how a theme tune becomes a ritual you look forward to, not one you sit through. She also looks at you. The pocket-and-stone gesture is aimed at the viewer as owner, so every episode ends with a small relationship moment, not a logo sting.

Her activities have the most physical comedy of the four and the clearest reads on a phone: hanging ten, cannonballing into a leaf pile, the kite lifting her off her flippers and her smoothing her fur as if nothing happened. The grooming tell gives every episode a button you can predict and still enjoy. And she has by far the strongest 'I want that' pull: a plush with a pocket and a stone is a toy with a secret, which is the DeRec idea you can hold in your hand.

Her real repeatability risk is the length of the locked ritual, so the grafts tighten it and make it musical, never longer. Pip is the smartest deadpan, but it is a shield with hands and has little warmth to wear in over time. Dee has the best tune and the best collectible, but she spends her first 1.5 seconds assembling instead of playing. Loop's snap is a transition, not a friend. Tuck, disciplined by Pip's deadpan and Dee's whistle, is the one people will still smile at in season three.

**Graft advice.** 1. FROM PIP, the deadpan non-disturbance rule, written into Tuck's animation bible as law: the carry-over item must survive the whole ritual untouched (the maple leaf stays on her head through the threshold chord, the baton stays behind her ear through pat-pat). The joke is that the ritual is so routine it disturbs nothing, and that joke is what carries the 60th viewing. Also take Pip's hush: a 2-frame stillness, with drums and bass dropping out, right before the first pat at f61, so the downbeat of bar two lands like a breath. And take Pip's host gesture: Bruce and Facundo pat the shield patch on their jackets twice to close each episode [HOST GATE]. That turns pat-pat into something the community does, not only something it watches.

2. FROM PIP, 'Pip's Year': tie each of Tuck's activities to the calendar month the episode airs, not to a fixed summer-to-summer order, so the regulars' own season matches hers.

3. FROM DEE, a whistleable motif. Tuck's four-hit sonic logo (pat, click, chord, pat-pat) can be tapped but not hummed. Voice the f91 threshold chord as a rising two-note figure on top, so the channel has a tune people can whistle as well as a rhythm they can tap. Also take the collectible logic: each season's twelve carry-over items become twelve enamel pins of Tuck ('Tuck with maple leaf', 'Tuck with baton'), and the plush ships with the grey stone in its pocket.

4. FROM LOOP, the rhyme between the activity and the ritual. Every bar-one button contains a double beat that foreshadows pat-pat: two drips off her nose in Ep01, two flaps of a hind flipper, the kite tugging twice. Regulars start hearing the ritual coming, and that anticipation is how a bumper becomes a ritual.

5. ONE CUT, so the fixed section doesn't wear out. Bar two stays at 60 frames and must never grow. Nobody may add beats to the locked master, and the 'new stones' variant is used at most once a season.

### Metaphor truth and brand safety: winner Loop

My lens is whether the image is true to threshold secret-sharing and whether it could embarrass the hosts or promise a guarantee. By that test Loop is the only pitch whose core picture is the actual maths rather than a friendly analogy. Shamir reconstruction is interpolating a curve through enough points, and Loop's snap is that. Below the threshold the cord is a random squiggle, never a partial figure. That is the one rule that kills the jigsaw myth, and Loop builds it into the animation rather than promising to police it. Facundo, who wrote the Rust crates, can call it 'Lagrange interpolation with eyes' and be right. The limits section even separates real-number geometry, which leaks, from a finite field, which doesn't. That is the rigour the AI-wary inventor will respect, and it is a picture he would find dignified rather than cute. On brand safety Loop is the cleanest: no altered shield, no protection iconography, no robot holding secrets, no animal facts to get wrong, no brand to collide with. Its visible flaws are the five-bead body parts, the two roles and the missing confirmation step. All three can be fixed with the specific grafts above, without changing the concept.

Tuck is second. Its metaphor discipline is excellent and in places better than Loop's: the faceless paws, the camera as owner, 'two never glow'. But the concept rests on otter biology full of [VERIFY] marks, it shows recovery in a helper's presence, and it puts an otter in front of an AI-wary inventor when the best-known otter brand is an AI company. Pip thinks about time more truthfully than anyone, but its body is the 'PROTECTED BY DeRec' shield and its species is 'robot holds your keys'. Both are overclaims my lens exists to catch, and it needs a disclaimer to survive. Dee lands a homing bird on the protection mark every episode, which is the purest form of the guarantee DeRec does not make, and she wears her shares as body parts.

Final call: Loop, with Dee's two waiting stars, Pip's owner-asks check-in and recognition beat, and Tuck's camera-is-the-owner staging grafted in.

**Graft advice.** 1. From Dee: keep the two beads that don't come at the frame edges. Fix Loop's worst flaw by dropping the idea that beads are hands, feet and heart. The three beads that answer land wherever they touch the cord, and that changes each episode, so 'any three' shows on screen. The snap makes Loop whole with only those three. Beads 4 and 5 never fly home: they stay dim at their own edges, like Dee's two stars, and take part only in the check-in. Draw hands and feet as plain cord curls. The 48px avatar becomes a gold ring over three amber dots on ink. This also takes the teeth out of the blockchain and necklace risk, because there is no longer an even row of five beads.
2. From Pip: the owner's device asks, and helpers answer where they are. Fix Loop's two roles by keeping Loop only as the thing that was recovered. In the check-in, the two muted plucks come from off-frame, as the owner's device asking, like Pip's chime. Each of the five beads (three on the cord, two at the edges) blinks back in its own place. No light ever runs from bead to bead, and the ripple travels only along the cord, which is the owner's thread. Loop's nod-bounce is the reaction, not the question. Also take Pip's line 'the glow is not a gauge': beads never brighten to mean 'more secure'.
3. From Pip: the moment of recognition. In the gather, each bead hangs for one hush frame, facing the cord, before it lands. That is the helper confirming it's really the owner, and it closes Loop's admitted gap (4) without adding time. In Ep01, Bruce asks 'why the little pause?' and Facundo explains the confirmation step [VERIFY against the spec].
4. From Tuck: the camera is the owner. The beads are held by off-screen hands that enter from fixed edges, never touch, and never turn toward each other. Say it in the bible: whoever watches is the owner, and only the owner sees all the beads. Also take Tuck's discipline: the hosts explain the metaphor once, as an aside in Ep01, and never again. Adopt Tuck's never-list as law: no storm, no thief, no guarding, no rescue.
5. From Pip: a spoken guardrail. Ep01 gets one line from the hosts: 'The beads are held by real people you choose. Loop's a drawing; the maths is real.' [HOST GATE]

### Production craft: winner Pip

My lens is whether a small team can make 24 or more of these a year and keep them on-model, readable on a phone and within 5 seconds.

Pip wins because the character is built so it cannot drift. It is rigid, it hovers, and it has no deformation, so every episode, whoever or whatever animates it, looks like the same Pip. Its bumper also follows the brief's north star better than any other pitch. It is one continuous shot with one small gag, and the check-in disturbs nothing, so the ritual stays calm instead of turning into a story. The timing works: the activity gets 66 frames with one gag, and the only beat under 10 frames is the 6-frame hush, which is a breath. The 120 BPM, 12-frame beat puts every hit on a whole frame. At 48px it reads as the shield logo alive, so avatar, end card, pin and figurine all come from one model. Its real cost is the sets, not the character, and the grafts from Dee and Tuck fix that.

Dee has the best locked-template discipline and the best merch. But it spends its 5 seconds on assembly, homecoming and lockup, so the activity gets 42 frames with five crammed actions, and Dee is too small in frame for a phone.

Loop is the cheapest per episode, but its rig works against staying on-model, it improvises props out of its own body by design, and its lockup gets only 6 frames.

Tuck is the most lovable at thumbnail size and the least producible. It needs a deforming 3D fur rig, costume assets and 2D-over-3D compositing, and its 'locked' ritual gets re-rendered every episode. Its threshold, the idea everything depends on, gets 8 frames, and its pat-pat is 3 frames apart and reads as one pat. That is the wrong pitch for a biweekly schedule and a small team.

**Graft advice.** Six changes to make before Pip goes to the client:

(1) Take Dee's two-ink scene system and rework Pip's sets. Each episode's background becomes a matte-painted 2.5D card in two inks chosen from a fixed six, sitting behind Pip's shallow depth of field. That replaces a fully modelled Eevee set every episode. It cuts set cost, which is where Pip's money goes, and stops the backgrounds drifting across 24 episodes.

(2) Take Tuck's mixed-media rule: effects are hand-drawn 2D, not simulation. The Kayak splash, the Sled snow spray and the kettle steam become 2D effects cards animated on twos. In Fetch, the dog becomes a painted 2D figure animated on twos, never a 3D fur rig. That keeps all four expensive activities affordable at 24 or more a year.

(3) Tighten Pip's own reuse claim into a spec. From f66 to f95, Pip always sits lower centre, three-quarter to camera-right, with the free mitt nearest the lens and the thread exiting frame right. The pat then becomes a single animation clip that drops onto every episode unchanged, and the end card (f96–119) is rendered once. Budget one set, one prop, about 66 frames of new animation and one re-render of the pat clip per episode.

(4) Take Loop's rule for small sizes: the avatar and icon always sit on an ink #123E32 circle, with no speckle or hands and a 2px gold outline. Also take Loop's and Dee's check: a scaled-down proportion sheet tested in greyscale at 48px and 24px before sign-off.

(5) Take Tuck's hit-point list format and its AI rule. Give the composer a frame-exact hit list: f0 downbeat, f36 grape tink, f66 hush, f72 and f78 chime and pats, f96 closing chord, f102 wordmark. Only the 66-frame activity backgrounds may be AI-assisted, and only if the hosts approve [HOST GATE], with disclosure. Pip itself and the pat are animated by hand and locked.

(6) Take Dee's merch logic. Make a speckled-stoneware Pip figurine at true 18cm mug scale, plus a 12-piece enamel pin set, one per activity.

### The client's taste: winner Tuck

Through the client's taste only: his north star is AI Code King's dancing pandas, a cute furry animal in polished 3D doing one joyful thing to a strong hook. His five chosen images are all golden-hour, saturated, sunlit and lush, with glowing amber accents and friendly white robots in the background. Tuck is the only concept that answers the pandas directly: a round, lovable mammal in warm painterly 3D. Her home silhouette is irresistible, her activities are joyful (surfing, a hot-air balloon, a lantern dance that pays tribute to the pandas), and she lives inside the world he already chose. The button eyes and the adult competence keep her 'classy', which is the half of his brief that stops a cute animal from sliding into a toddler show. He grins at Tuck. Pip is the runner-up because it matches his literal words (bot, helper, organizational logo) and his cover's robots. But a rigid porcelain shield with a one-line face and dry, deadpan jokes delivers 'clever and on-brand', not 'fun, endearing, warm'. The recurring robot sidekick grafted into Tuck's world keeps what he'd like about Pip. Dee is elegant, and landing on the shield is a lovely brand payoff. But flat 2D heraldry in midnight navy moves back toward the restrained 'classic' look he already said was too sleepy, and away from the luminous 3D he loves. Loop is the one he'd politely hate: abstract, faceless, craft-fair, with no future in it and nothing like a panda. The fixes that turn his grin into a firm yes are: a bot friend beside Tuck, a guaranteed 'tomorrow' detail in every plate, backgrounds as saturated as his references, stones that light up like his café gems, and more screen time for the adventure than for the ceremony.

**Graft advice.** 1) From Pip, put the bot back into his vocabulary without replacing Tuck. Give Tuck one recurring white-and-cyan robot sidekick, a small café-bot built with Pip's personality rules (height is mood, a bending face-line, a ceramic 'tink'). It appears in the ensemble activities (Ep04 chess, Ep07 bells, Ep10 repotting, Ep12 dance) and is always the robot paw in the ritual rotation. That answers his 'DeRec bot / DeRec helper' line and brings in the agentic, kind-machine future he asked for. 2) From Pip, take the 'one tomorrow detail per bumper' rule: an air taxi, an autonomous shuttle or a crystalline skyline in every bar-one plate, so the touch of modernity is guaranteed and never left to chance. 3) Reject Pip's 60% background desaturation, and Tuck's mint-washed grade for the environments. Keep his saturated turquoise water, blue sky and sunlit greenery from references 00–04 in the plates, and keep blue off Tuck herself only. He chose lush images; give him lush. 4) From Loop and Dee, the threshold moment must glow like his café image. Make the lit stones read as the amber gems of 04 (#F2A23A/#FFD68A inner glow, Dee's facet shadow) the instant they touch, so his favourite picture visibly comes alive. At rest they stay plain slate. 5) Tighten Tuck's bar two for fun. Cut the fixed ritual from 2.5s to about 1.75s by overlapping the pocket cue with the button of the activity. That gives the adventure about 3s, because the adventure is what he'll rewatch. 6) From Dee, make the sonic logo something people can whistle, not just the four hits: add a two-note rising motif on the threshold chord so it works as the audio-only ident. 7) Use Dee's enamel-pin idea for merch: Tuck's twelve activity poses as a collectible pin set for Alliance events [HOST GATE].

## Tuck

*Animal lane.* A sea otter in storybook-style 3D who is one of your helpers. She keeps your share as a plain grey stone in the real pocket of loose skin under her left forearm, and every bumper ends with her bringing it out.

> Every cold open, Tuck the sea otter is off doing something new, and every one ends the same way: she pats the pocket under her arm, holds up the plain little stone she keeps for you, and when two unseen friends bring theirs, the three ordinary stones light up together.

### Look

**Silhouette.** A dense, buoyant bean. Standing, she is 2.6 heads tall. Her round head is slightly wider than tall, over soft sloping shoulders and a full chest. Her short forelimbs end in mitten paws, true to the species: no fingers to animate, so no AI finger errors either. Oversized webbed hind flippers do the comic timing, and she has a short, flat paddle of a tail. Her home pose is the classic sea-otter float: on her back, head up, paws together on her chest. That shape is recognisable at any size.

**Face.** A cream mask covers her muzzle, cheeks and brow and spills onto her throat; older sea otters really do go pale around the head. Her eyes are small, glossy, set wide and low, each with one gold catchlight. Button eyes, not anime eyes: small eyes read as adult and classy. Her nose is a broad, rounded ink triangle. She has seven pale whiskers per side, thick at the root, trailing behind her moves (cheap secondary delight). Her ears are tiny, low nubs.

**Wardrobe.** One item, always: a ribbon of kelp worn as a scarf, tied once on her left, ending in a small kelp float bulb (the real air bladder of giant kelp). It's her colour pop, it gives the eye a dot at 48px, and it nods to the true anti-drift behaviour. No shield patch on Tuck: the brand lives on the end card, so she stays a character rather than a uniform. Each episode allows exactly one carry-over item into the ritual shot (listed in the activities).

**Pocket and stone.** The pocket is suggested by a soft fold of loose skin under her left forearm, with a small visible bump where the stone sits. It is always the left [VERIFY the left preference before saying it on camera]. The stone is a smooth, flattened egg of matte slate, about three of her noses long, with pale speckles and one tiny chip on its edge. The chip is how regulars know it's hers. The helpers' stones are the same grey in different plain shapes (round, flat, oblong). Stones are never coin-shaped, never stamped, and never gold at rest.

**Palette (show palette plus two fur tones).**
- Fur: #5A3D2B warm cocoa, core shadow #3A281D
- Face and bib: #EFE2C8 cream, echoing the cover's ivory jacket
- Whiskers and highlights: #F7EEDC
- Eyes and nose: #1C2B25, from the ink family
- Kelp scarf: #1B7466 jade, highlight #2F8F7D, folds #123E32 ink
- Stone: #8E948F slate, underside #6F7571
- Threshold light: #B18C44 gold core with #E9C878 glow
- End-card paper: #F7F8F1
- Sky fill and atmosphere: #DCF4E9 mint
No blue anywhere on the character (see risks).

**Materials.** The fur is sculpted, not simulated: clumps are carved into the mesh and hand-painted, with soft rim-lit peach fuzz. That gives felted-toy warmth for a fraction of the cost of a groom sim. Nose, ears and paw pads get soft subsurface scattering. The stone is matte and dry, with a faint wet sheen only in water scenes. The kelp is waxy and slightly translucent.

**Rendering style: modern 3D with storybook warmth.**
- Stylised 3D, with the character animated on ones at 24 fps.
- An NPR contour line in ink #123E32 at about 50% opacity, 1–2 px at 1080p, heavier on the shadow side. This is the "printed book" line.
- Always a warm golden key light, a cream bounce, jade-tinted shadows and a mint sky fill. Bloom only on the gold.
- In the composite: 4% scanned paper grain in #F7F8F1 and a soft vignette.
- Environments are matte-painted 2.5D cards of the reference world: white terraces, greenery, waterfalls, glass, small white-and-cyan robots.
- The threshold threads are hand-drawn 2D on twos, with a gentle line boil. They are visibly a different medium, so the magic never reads as biology.
Touchstones: the painterly 3D of The Wild Robot, lit like a Beatrix Potter watercolour.

**What makes it read at 48px.** Four shapes: a cream disc (the face), two dark dots and a nose, a jade band beneath, and a grey oval held at the chin. In greyscale the cream is about 7.6:1 against the cocoa, so the face reads without colour. The jade scarf sits between the cream face and the cream bib (about 4.4:1), not on the brown fur, where it would vanish. The avatar and icon versions drop the contour line and the grain. Everything is composed inside the centre 9:16 safe area, so the same master crops to Shorts.

### Personality

**Unflappable, competent, quietly delighted.** Tuck takes up a new hobby each episode and does it like a master craftsperson on her day off: full commitment, a small flourish when it goes right, no mugging. She is both hosts in one small body. She has Bruce's spark: she'll try anything and visibly loves it. She has Facundo's steady hand: precise, calm, and at most one dry glance to the lens per bumper. When something goes wrong she recovers with dignity, smooths her cheek fur and carries on. Grooming is her tell. Sea otters groom constantly because their fur is their only insulation, so a quick smooth of the cheek means both "that went well" and "I'm fine." She's the friend who holds on to something for you and never asks what it is.

**Acting notes.** Dense and buoyant: heavier than she looks on land (she settles, waddles a little, her flippers slap) and lighter than air in water. Her forepaws are mittens, so she grips with both. Every hold looks careful and two-handed, like carrying something precious. Her hind flippers carry the comedy: a flap of satisfaction, a curl of concentration. Her whiskers trail everything.

**She never:** talks (foley and music only), panics, winks (too salesy), scowls, guards or fights anything, faces a predator or a storm (no fear), eats on camera (sea otters eat crabs and urchins, so no prey, ever), uses her stone as a tool, or looks at the mark.

She is written as "she" because female southern sea otters are the keener stone-users (Law et al., Science, 2024). It's a true detail the hosts can enjoy.

### Signature move

**The pocket pat.** 30 frames (1.25s at 24 fps), identical in every episode. The ritual cue just before it ends with one felted pat on her pocket.
- **Frames 1–6:** from the pocket shot, she draws her stone out from under her left forearm and lifts it to the lens in both mitten paws. It's dull and plain. (Stone click.)
- **Frames 7–14:** two other paws slide in with their own plain stones, one from frame left and one from top right. These edges never change. The paws never touch each other, and there are no faces.
- **Frames 15–22:** the three stones touch, and at that instant all three light amber. Fine gold threads, hand-drawn on twos (four drawings), snap between them and draw the return-arrow D in the space at their centre. (Threshold chord.) Tuck's eyes stay on you; she never looks at the mark.
- **Frames 23–30:** the paws withdraw the way they came, their stones dimming as they go. Tuck tucks her stone back under her arm and pats the pocket twice, pat-pat. The pat-pat is the pickup into the final downbeat, where the D becomes the shield lockup.

**Sonic logo.** Four hits: the pat at the cue, the stone click, the chord, then pat-pat. It's a motif the channel can reuse for Shorts, transitions and notifications.

**Rules.**
- One stone never glows. Two never glow. The third always completes it.
- The joining paws rotate quietly among Tuck's four friends: Gran's silver-grizzled paw, a chocolate paw, a sandy paw, and the café robot's white-and-cyan hand (businesses can be helpers too). At a glance the ritual looks identical every time; regulars who look closer notice that any three will do.

**Why it sticks.** It's a real otter gesture. It happens right at the lens, where you are the owner. And it ends on a rhythm anyone can tap on a desk.

### How it maps to DeRec, and where the mapping stops

**Fact check first. Both facts in the premise needed adjusting.**
1. *Rafts and paw-holding: half true.* Sea otters do rest in groups called rafts, often split by sex, from a handful of animals to hundreds. Paw-holding is real but occasional. The famous "otters holding hands" clip shows two captive otters, Nyac and Milo, filmed at the Vancouver Aquarium in 2002 and posted in 2007. In the wild, a sleeping otter usually stops itself drifting by wrapping itself in kelp. The true version the hosts can say: "Sea otters sometimes hold paws while they float so they don't drift apart, though mostly they wrap themselves in kelp." [VERIFY with a sea-otter biologist, e.g. Monterey Bay Aquarium's sea otter programme, before recording.]
2. *The pocket is true. The favourite stone is overstated.* Sea otters have a loose fold of skin under each forearm. They stash food from a dive there, plus a rock they use as a tool. Many sources say they prefer the left one [VERIFY]. But "each otter keeps one favourite stone, sometimes for life" is internet lore. Otters often reuse one rock through a single feeding bout. Researcher Jessica Fujii, quoted in Hakai Magazine: "they use a rock, and then they drop it." Tool use also varies. It happens on a minority of dives in Monterey [VERIFY: about 16% of successful dives], and females use tools more than males (Law et al., Science, 2024). So Tuck keeping one stone is her choice as a character, not a claim about otters. Nobody says "otters keep a favourite rock for life" on camera.

**What the fact check forces: keep the pocket, and drop the raft from the ritual.** The raft is the wrong half of the otter for DeRec. A chain of otters holding paws links the helpers to each other, and that is exactly what DeRec prevents. The pocket is the right half: one small, plain thing, kept privately on your own body, meaningless on its own. So the ritual shows no raft. The paw-holding fact survives as a line the hosts can say, and in the kelp scarf Tuck wears (kelp is the real anchor).

**How each DeRec property maps:**
- *A single share reveals nothing.* Tuck's stone is plain, blank, matte grey. One stone alone never glows. Two stones touching never glow either: that is collusion, and it gets you nothing. The stones are never jigsaw pieces. A jigsaw piece shows part of the picture, and you need every piece. A Shamir share shows nothing, and any enough will do. HOLDS, by design.
- *Each helper keeps their share on their own device.* The stone lives in Tuck's own pocket, which is real otter anatomy. HOLDS, and it's true.
- *A threshold restores the secret.* The instant a third stone touches the other two, all three light amber, like the gems on the café table in 04. Gold threads then draw the return-arrow D between them. Which two friends join Tuck changes quietly from episode to episode: any three of five, where five is the helper circle in the bible. INVENTED: no otter does this. So it's drawn in a different visual language, hand-drawn 2D gold animated on twos over 3D animated on ones, and reads as magic, not zoology. Three-of-five is only illustrative [VERIFY with Facundo that the hosts describe thresholds the way lib-derec actually configures them].
- *Helpers don't know about each other.* The other helpers appear only as paws, entering from two fixed frame edges. No faces. They never touch, and they leave the way they came. Tuck never looks at them. Only the camera sees all three, and the camera is you, the owner. STAGED: real rafts are social.
- *Helpers never learn the secret.* Tuck never looks at the mark. Her eyes stay on you. She doesn't know what your stone opens, and doesn't need to. HOLDS.
- *Quiet daily check-ins.* The pat on the pocket means "still got it." APPROXIMATE: in DeRec the helper does nothing, because the owner's device checks the helper's device automatically. The pat is a human stand-in for a silent machine habit.
- *Re-sharing when things change.* A one-off variant of the ritual, "new stones": the old stones go dull and sand-coloured, and a fresh stone is handed to Tuck from off-frame. Use it only when something actually changes, so it stays honest. The recommendation is season two, episode one. INVENTED.

**Exactly where it stops being true.**
(1) Otters don't combine stones. The lighting-up is ours.
(2) A real otter's stone is a hammer or anvil for smashing shells. Tuck's stone never hits anything, because smashing reads as breaking in.
(3) Rafts are social and DeRec helpers aren't. The staging corrects for this; the animal doesn't.
(4) The pat suggests the helper has a chore. In DeRec they don't.
(5) The mark at the centre stands for "what comes back", not for the secret itself.

**Stress-testing the alternatives.** No animal does secret sharing. So the test was: whose true behaviour covers the most of DeRec honestly, and leaves the smallest, most clearly fictional part to invent?
- *Meerkats standing sentry.* Brilliant pop-up animation, wrong message. A sentry watches for danger and raises the alarm: that's threat monitoring. It suggests DeRec detects attacks, which it doesn't (non-negotiable 1), and it opens the show on fear. Rejected.
- *Emperor-penguin huddle.* The only candidate with a real "enough bodies" threshold: a huddle needs numbers to hold its heat. But no penguin holds anything private, everyone touches everyone, and the Antarctic palette fights our golden world. Crowd animation is expensive, the image is survival in a storm, and penguin mascots are worn out (Tux, Pingu). Rejected.
- *Honeyguides and human honey-hunters.* True and lovely trust between species: Yao and Hadza honey-hunters answer the bird's call and follow it to bees' nests. But it's two different roles that each need the other (two of two), not shares of one secret. The partners know each other, and the payoff is breaking open a bee colony. It would turn specific communities into a mascot's backdrop, and the bird is a small brown speck at 48px. Better as an anecdote in a trust episode. Rejected as mascot.
- *Elephants circling a calf.* A ring of bodies guarding the treasure is custody, not sharing, and it implies helpers hold the secret itself. "Elephants never forget" contradicts "helpers learn nothing". There's a US political-party association, and too much mass for a one-second gag. Rejected.
- *Sociable weaver birds.* Each pair has its own private chamber inside one shared nest, which is an honest picture of shares held separately. But there's no recovery moment, weaving is too slow to read in a second, and the bird vanishes at thumbnail size. Keep it as b-roll. Rejected.
- *Bonus: honeybee house-hunting.* Bees use a literal quorum threshold: the swarm moves once enough scouts gather at one site [VERIFY numbers]. But it's a vote, not a secret, and a swarm reads as a threat. Rejected.

**Why the otter wins.** Its two true behaviours cover the human half of DeRec: a circle of people you trust, and one small thing, meaningless alone, kept privately on your own body. The signature gesture, reaching into the pocket, is real anatomy. Floating on its back is one of the most recognisable animal silhouettes there is, at any size. Otters' reputation for play earns "the world's the limit". And the one thing we invent is small, clearly marked as magic, and lifted straight from the client's favourite reference image.

### Risks

**Twee is the biggest risk.** Otters are maximum-cute, and one wrong step turns this into a toddler show or a plush-toy ad. The guard is restraint:
- an adult heroine who is good at things
- button eyes, not saucer eyes
- no voice, no winks, no bows, no pup sidekick
- only the jade, gold and cream palette
- an editorial camera and a paper end card.
The humour stays dry, the way Facundo would do it.

**Overclaiming the biology.** "Otters keep a favourite stone for life" is lore, and "otters hold hands to sleep" overstates something occasional. The hosts use only the corrected lines from the metaphor section. [VERIFY with a sea-otter biologist before Ep01 records.]

**Overclaiming DeRec.** Four rules, all built into the ritual template:
1. One stone never glows, and neither do two.
2. The stones are never jigsaw pieces.
3. Helpers never show a face on screen.
4. Tuck never guards, fights or rescues anything. No storm, no orca, no thief: nothing that implies DeRec stops hacks, scams or theft (non-negotiable 1), and no fear-selling.
Stones are never coin-like and never gold at rest (non-negotiable 6). Hosts should explain DeRec through otters once, as an aside in Ep01, and never again.

**Off-brand collisions.**
- Otter.ai is a well-known AI note-taking company that uses an otter identity. On a trust show whose protocol inventor is wary of AI, even a whiff of sponsorship would be a problem. Keep blue off Tuck entirely, and never pair her with meeting or transcription imagery.
- Keep member-company colours off her too, so DeRec never reads as any single member.
- Ferris, the Rust crab mascot, is public domain and tempting as a nod to Facundo's crowd. But sea otters eat crabs. Never put Ferris next to Tuck; the community will make that joke for you.

**Conservation.** The southern sea otter is listed as threatened under the US Endangered Species Act, and sea otters are Endangered on the IUCN Red List. Never show people hand-feeding, petting or "owning" otters. Any conservation link in the video description is a [HOST GATE] decision because of the neutral-space rule.

**The raft temptation.** Someone will want a cute raft of otters holding paws in the ritual. Refuse. It shows helpers linked to each other, which is exactly what DeRec avoids. Rafts may appear only as scenery inside an activity, and never with stones.

**Cost.** Fur and water are the two most expensive things in CG.
- Fur: sculpted, painted fur with no groom simulation.
- Water: a painted displacement wave plus 2D spray, never a fluid sim. Only Ep01 needs water in bar one, and the ritual has none.
- Template: one hero rig, one paw rig with four texture sets, and one locked 60-frame ritual master. Each new episode is about 2.5s of new animation plus one matte-painted background.
- Land activities need one reusable waddle-and-settle cycle, because animals with flippers walk awkwardly.

**AI production.** If bar one is AI-generated, it will fail in exactly the places the metaphor depends on: extra digits, a vanishing stone, the pocket switching sides, paws bunching together. The ritual master must be animated by hand and locked. Only bar one may be AI-assisted, and any AI use gets disclosed on YouTube and in the description [HOST GATE].

**The name.** "Tuck" is the verb of her signature move. It's one syllable, easy for English and Spanish speakers, and collides with nothing that matters. [HOST GATE] before any trademark or merchandise use.

## Pip

*Helper-machine lane.* One mug-sized DeRec helper made of speckled porcelain, shaped like a softened DeRec shield. It has one cyan line for a face and one amber bead (its share) glowing in a small window on its chest.

> Every episode, a mug-sized porcelain robot named Pip is off doing something small and delightful, like riding a penny-farthing, conducting kettles or holding a leaf over a snail, and every episode, without missing a beat, it pats the one amber bead in its chest the way you pat your pocket for your keys: still got it.

### Look

WHERE IT COMES FROM: The white-and-cyan service robots give it a white body, a dark visor and cyan light. The gold return-arrow shield gives it its outline, a gold rim and gold-on-dark accents. The café constellation gives it amber.

SILHOUETTE: A softened shield: a flat top with rounded shoulders, tapering to a blunt point. The mascot is the DeRec shield brought to life. It is one piece, with no head, no neck and no legs. It hovers, which means it never needs a walk cycle. Its flat top works like a tray, a nod to the cover's coffee-serving robots, and things land on it. Two separate mitten hands float beside it and hold each episode's prop.

PROPORTIONS (H = body height, 18 cm in the show's world, the size of a coffee mug):
- Shoulder width 0.86H.
- Flat top 0.62H wide, with 0.12H corner radii.
- Bottom point radius 0.18H.
- Visor: a capsule 0.52H x 0.20H, with its top edge 0.12H below the top of the body.
- Face-line: one stroke 0.025H thick and 0.24H long.
- Chest window: 0.16H across, centred 0.58H down, holding a 0.11H bead.
- Mitts: 0.20H long, floating 0.10H off the body, each with a 0.03H gold cuff.
- Hover gap: 0.15H.
- In every shot Pip fills at least 30% of the frame height.

PALETTE:
- Glaze white #FBF8EF.
- Iron speckle #8C7B66, covering about 3% of the surface.
- Rim and cuffs in brushed gold #B18C44, highlight #E6BE62.
- Visor and window glass in ink #12332B.
- Face-line and hover glow in cyan #46E0D2.
- Bead in amber #F5A623, with a #FFD27A core and a #C9781E edge.
- The world around Pip keeps the prior package's jade #1B7466, mint #DCF4E9 and paper #F7F8F1.
- Only two things on Pip glow: the face-line and the bead. Nothing on Pip is ever red.

MATERIALS: Speckled stoneware glaze with a satin finish (roughness about 0.35), taken from the speckled mug on the café table in 04. It reads as handmade and warm, and it is the biggest single step away from the glossy-plastic EVE family the reference robots belong to. A hairline gold seam traces the outline, like the shield's rim. The visor and window are dark glass. No chrome, screws, panel lines, antenna, camera lens or LED strips.

RENDERING: Stylised 3D in Blender (Eevee), finished to feel like a printed book:
- A soft golden key light from camera-left, with warm bounce.
- A 4% paper-grain overlay.
- A gentle glow bleed on the two lights only.
- Shallow depth of field, so the solarpunk backgrounds from the references become soft painted washes at about 60% saturation.

Pip is rigid and never deforms. One model keeps its identity across every episode, and the rig is just four objects, one bendable curve and one glowing element.

READING AT 48px: At that size you see the logo: a white rounded shield, a dark band with a cyan "^", and an amber dot in the middle of the body. For the icon version, drop the speckle and hands, and replace the hairline rim with a 2px gold outline. The bead sits inside a ring of dark glass, so it reads as a light rather than a hole or a mouth. Cyan on ink is about 8.4:1 contrast. Amber on ink is about 6.7:1. Amber directly on the white glaze would be only about 1.9:1, which is why the window glass is dark.

### Personality

THE NAME: A pip is a seed, a point of light, and the short beep of a radio time signal. That makes it something small you keep, something that glows, and a daily "still here". It is one syllable and easy to say in English and Spanish. On screen it appears as "Pip, the DeRec helper".

WHO IT IS: Discreet, diligent and easily delighted. Kind in small ways. Pip has a full life, with twelve hobbies a season. Keeping its bead is such a settled habit that it never interrupts anything, which is the protocol's promise to helpers: helping costs you nothing day to day.
- It never peeks at its bead. It couldn't learn anything if it did.
- It takes small things seriously, like a quarter-turn of a screw or the leaf in a latte, and big things lightly: when a kite lifts it off the ground, it lets itself be carried.
- Its humour comes from scale and deadpan. Something lands on its flat top with a "tink", the face-line goes flat for eight frames, and it carries on.
- From the hosts: Bruce's spark is Pip rising when delighted. Facundo's steady hand is its unhurried, exact pat.
- It never speaks.

HOW IT MOVES: Personality comes from movement, under four rules.
1. Height is mood. It floats up when pleased, sinks when concentrating, and drops and recovers when startled.
2. Tilt is attention. It has no neck, so it leans its whole body toward whatever it cares about.
3. Hands lead, body follows. The mitts arrive 2–3 frames before the body and settle 2–3 frames after it.
4. Stillness is listening. There is a 2-frame hush before every pat.

When idle, it bobs 2% of its height over 48 frames. The bob slows when Pip concentrates and stops completely when it sits in a vehicle.

THE FACE: The face-line has four shapes:
- "—" for focus and deadpan
- "·" for curious or startled
- "~" for effort
- "^" for content

The line bends but never moves side to side, because a sideways sweep reads as a scanner. The "^" appears only once per bumper, at the end of the pocket pat, so Pip's happiest face lands on the same beat every episode. Its one look to camera happens with its eyes closed, so it never stares.

SOUND: No voice. A ceramic "tink", tuned to the music's key, when anything lands on its top. The two-note chime belongs to the music: it is the owner's device asking, not Pip talking.

### Signature move

THE POCKET PAT: 3.00s–4.00s (24 frames), identical every episode. It is the gesture everyone makes when they pat a pocket for their keys: quietly checking you still have the thing, without stopping what you're doing.

- f0 (3.00s), chime note 1: Without breaking stride, Pip's free mitt pats its chest window. The bead flares from 60% to 100% and back to 60% over 5 frames.
- f6 (3.25s), chime note 2: A second pat, with the same flare.
- f10–f15 (3.42s–3.63s): The face-line springs from "—" to "^" with a 2-frame overshoot. Pip rises 6% of its height and tilts 4° toward camera. This is its only look at us, and it does it with its eyes closed.
- f14–f24 (3.58s–4.00s): A hairline gold thread unspools from the window and whips out of frame right. That is the reply going home. The bead stays put.

WHY THIS GESTURE:
- A 70-year-old can read it in a single frame.
- It is true to the protocol: daily, quiet, and the share never leaves.
- It is cheap: one mitt, one curve and one glowing element, laid over any activity.
- Real people can do it. Bruce and Facundo could close each episode by patting the gold shield patch on their jackets twice, which sits exactly where Pip's window does [HOST GATE]. The community could do it on the fortnightly contributors call. The show's signature becomes a gesture people do themselves, not only a logo they watch.

### How it maps to DeRec, and where the mapping stops

DECISION: ONE PIP ON SCREEN, ALWAYS ALONE IN FRAME. A flock of bots sharing a frame, each carrying a glowing piece, looks more faithful at first. In five seconds it teaches the wrong lesson. Bots that can see each other and work together contradict the protocol's most surprising property: helpers don't know who the other helpers are, or how many there are. So the flock is real, but it never meets. Editorial law: no two Pips ever share a frame.

HOW IT MAPS
- Shares reveal nothing alone: Pip's bead is plain and smooth, with no facets, no picture and no jigsaw edge, and it looks the same every episode. There is nothing on it to read because there is nothing in one share to learn. (The 04 café image uses cut gems. We deliberately simplify them to beads, because a jewel says "treasure" and a lone share is worth nothing to a thief.)
- Daily quiet check-ins: the pocket pat. The two-note chime is the owner's device asking. Pip's two pats and the gold thread that follows are the answer. The bead never leaves Pip's chest. Only "still here" travels.
- Helpers unaware of each other: on the end card, Pip's thread arrives alongside two fainter threads from other edges of the frame. They meet only there, on the owner's side, after we have left Pip's world.
- A threshold restores: this never happens in the weekly bumper, because the check-in is daily and recovery is rare. It gets one special, "The Gathering", for the trailer or season finale. Six split-screen panels show six Pips in six worlds, none aware of the others. The owner asks. Each Pip looks up to recognise the owner, and four of the six send their beads [VERIFY the default threshold in lib-derec]. The beads meet in the owner's open hand and join into a small constellation by threads of light, which completes the 04 café image. One Pip is asleep and one is off-panel mid-paddle, because recovery doesn't need everyone. This is the only place beads ever travel.
- Re-sharing when things change: the "New bead" variant, for a season premiere or an episode about changing helpers. A fresh bead arrives on a thread and settles into the window. On the same beat the old one dims to nothing, and Pip gets on with its day. [VERIFY that earlier shares stop working after a re-share.]

WHERE THE MAPPING STOPS BEING TRUE (enforce these in the art, and say them on camera if asked)
1. A share is not a piece of the secret. Shamir shares are not fragments, and one bead reveals nothing at all, not even a little. Any picture, glyph or facet on a bead breaks the metaphor.
2. Helpers are people and organisations you choose, not robots. Pip stands in for a helper and the helper's phone together. DeRec involves no AI agents, and no autonomous machine holds anyone's secret. Pip is a character, not a part of the product.
3. The real check-in is invisible. The helper does nothing, and their device answers on its own. The pat is theatre that lets us see a background process. [VERIFY the spec's wording for how a helper's device proves it still holds a share without handing the share over.]
4. Recovery has a human step that Pip can't show in five seconds: helpers confirm it's really the owner before releasing a share. [VERIFY the exact flow.] The Gathering shows it with each Pip's look of recognition.
5. The shield body is brand lineage, not a promise. DeRec is recovery, not protection. It doesn't undo a hack, reverse a transaction you authorised, or stop a scam. Pip never guards, blocks, fights or fends anything off. Pip keeps things; it doesn't defend them.
6. The glow is not a gauge. The bead never brightens to mean "more secure" or dims to mean "at risk".

## Dee, the Alliance martlet

*Living-emblem lane.* A heraldic barn swallow made of midnight enamel, gold wire and amber stars. She is the living crest of an imagined Alliance coat of arms: every episode she assembles herself from three of five stars, does that episode's activity, and flies home to perch on top of the official DeRec shield.

> Three stars that mean nothing on their own fly in and become Dee, a little enamel swallow who does one delightful new thing each episode and then flies home to perch on the DeRec shield, so every episode opens by recovering itself.

### Look

SILHOUETTE: a barn swallow drawn the way a martlet appears on a crest. Compact teardrop body. A big round head, 40% of body length. Short sickle wings that fold into a crossed-scissor X over the tail at rest. A deep forked tail whose two streamers run 0.8 of a body length past the fork, each tipped with a small amber star. The fork is non-negotiable: it's what makes her read as a swallow at any size. Tiny black beak. One round black eye set high and forward with a single white catchlight. The eye is never wider than the beak is long, so she reads friendly rather than babyish. Her feet are two neat gold hooks. Her three amber gems sit at the throat and the two tail tips; they are the three stars that assembled her, so she wears her threshold.

PROPORTIONS: 1 body length (B), measured beak to fork. Wingspan open 2.4B. Height when perched 0.9B. She fills about 22% of frame height during the activity and about 30% when perched on the shield.

PALETTE
- Midnight enamel #15223A: cap, back, wings.
- Swallow sheen #34598A: gloss sweep on back and wings, standing in for the real bird's blue iridescence.
- Ivory #F6EEDB: belly and underwing.
- Heart amber #F2A23A, with glow #FFD68A and facet shadow #B8651E: all gems. The throat gem stands in for the barn swallow's rust throat.
- Crest gold #C9A24D, highlight #F1D58A: keylines and feet only.
- Stage: Paper #F7F8F1 and Ink #123E32, carried over from the prior package on purpose so the "classic" half survives.
- Scene inks, two per episode, chosen from a fixed six: Sky #8CCDE6, Lake #2F8F9D, Leaf #6FAF6A, Lavender #A898D8, Coral #EE8A62, Dusk #3B3F7A. This gives variety within one family.

MATERIALS: Dee is a living cloisonné enamel pin. Her colours are flat enamel cells divided by raised gold wire. That wire is the keyline, in the same gold as the shield rim and the small patch on the hosts' jackets. One glassy highlight slides across her as she turns, and her gems are faceted amber glass that glows from inside. The threads of light are fine gold filaments with a soft bloom, borrowed from the orbit of light on the cover. The threads and the gems are the only glow in the frame, so the eye always lands on them.

RENDERING: 2D vector. The scenes are cut paper and screen print, in the lineage of Saul Bass and 1950s travel posters, with a 1–2px registration offset and 4% paper grain. That's the "printed book about tomorrow". Dee animates on 1s (24 fps) and the scenes on 2s, the classic TV-animation trick that makes a character pop off a printed world. No 3D, no photoreal, and no AI-rendered frames in the final.

WHY SHE READS AT 48px
1. The forked tail is exaggerated and never thinner than 3px at 48px.
2. She is a dark silhouette on light paper, and a 2px gold keyline rescues her on dark or busy backgrounds.
3. The amber throat gem is the brightest pixel in any frame.
Avatar pose: perched three-quarter view, fork open, facets and sheen removed. Test in grayscale at 48px and 24px before sign-off.

BONUS: she already looks like an enamel pin, so the twelve activities make a ready-made collectible set for Alliance events.

### Personality

Dee is the regular who's up for anything and always home on time.
- Cheerfully competent. She's good at things. The comedy comes from scale and commitment, never from failure or slapstick pain: a bird knitting a scarf four times her length with total seriousness.
- Dry, not zany. Her reactions are understated: small nods, a slow head-tilt. She has Bruce's spark in the air and Facundo's precision on landing, so the hosts' partnership lives inside her without the hosts appearing.
- Warm and sociable. The little white-and-cyan robots from the cover adore her, and she treats them as friends, never as servants. That's the brief's "helpful little machines doing small, kind things".
- One harmless vanity: she's proud of her tail. She preens it now and then and paints it too long in her self-portrait. A flaw is what makes a mascot lovable rather than corporate.
- Punctual and tidy, the way real swallows return on schedule. She embodies the ritual.
- She never speaks, only her two-note call and body language, so she needs no translation for a global Alliance audience.
- Never frightened, never fighting, never guarding, never handling money.
- She is "she": two men host the show, and its third character shouldn't make it three.
Her joy is flight; her comfort is home.

### Signature move

THE HOMECOMING (about 1.0s, 3.50s–4.50s).
1. She lifts off from whatever she's doing.
2. She flies one swooping curl that turns back on itself in the shape of the return arrow in the DeRec "D", trailing a hairline of gold.
3. She lands on the top edge of the shield and folds her wings into their crossed-scissor rest.
4. On the downbeat she flicks her forked tail open and shut: "tik".
5. She sings her two-note call, "dee-REC", low to high, and her throat gem pulses on "REC".

WHY THIS AND NOT A WAVE OR A WINK: it's a shape a viewer can draw with a finger and a call they can whistle. And it lands on the Alliance's mark every time, so the brand is the last thing on screen without needing a separate logo sting.

The name comes from the call. The call is also the sonic logo: it opens the bumper as the owner's call and closes it as Dee's answer. Reuse it to close each episode and as the ident for any audio-only feed.

### How it maps to DeRec, and where the mapping stops

DECISION: a creature from an imagined crest, made out of the constellation motif, who comes home to the official shield. I ruled out the other two. A shield with a face and legs trivialises the Alliance's trust mark. It also can't do twelve activities without turning into a logo with limbs, and it puts a personality on a badge that says "protected", which is overclaim territory. The constellation on its own is beautiful but has no face: it's the material, not the character. I chose a swallow because its whole folklore is about coming home, and because the barn swallow's natural colours already match the shield: blue-black, rust-gold and cream. In heraldic terms she is "a martlet between five mullets", meaning a swallow among five stars. When she lands on top of the shield she literally becomes its crest, because in heraldry the crest is the figure that sits on top.

HOW IT MAPS
- A single share reveals nothing. The first gem lands and nothing happens. The second lands, a thread flickers between them and dies. All the gems look identical and have no features, so none of them holds a piece of the bird.
- A threshold restores. When the third gem lands, one curve closes through all three and Dee pours in along it. The gems are points and the bird is drawn through them. That's the honest intuition behind Shamir's scheme: enough points pin down one curve. It avoids the jigsaw myth, where each share is a piece of the picture. Five stars wait at the edges and only three answer, so you don't need everyone. Suggested live beat for Ep01: Bruce asks why only three came, and Facundo's answer explains the threshold in words Grandma can follow.
- Helpers don't know about each other. The five stars sit in separate corners with no lines between them, and their flight paths never cross. The only thread that ever joins them runs through Dee, who stands for the owner.
- Daily quiet check-ins. As she lands, the five edge stars twinkle once in sequence, a half-second roll call. Her idle loop on end cards is the long version: she glances at each star in turn and each one twinkles back.
- Re-sharing when things change. Once a season, on the "keeping recovery current" episode, one edge star fades and a new one appears somewhere else. From then on the new five are the default. This is the only change to the ritual that is allowed. [HOST GATE]

WHERE IT STOPS BEING TRUE (the hosts should know these cold)
1. Recovery doesn't start itself and isn't instant. The owner asks from a new device, and each helper should confirm it's really the owner before releasing a share [VERIFY how the spec describes helper verification]. The bumper skips that human step.
2. Mathematically, anyone holding fewer shares than the threshold learns nothing. But regulars know Dee, so two gems already look like her. Familiarity gives away what the maths doesn't.
3. The "curve" in Shamir's scheme is a polynomial over a finite field, not a line you could draw. The thread is an intuition, not a diagram.
4. 3-of-5 is only an illustration. The real threshold is "roughly half" and is set by the owner's app [VERIFY the default before anyone says a number on camera].
5. She brings back what you lost, not what was stolen. Dee never chases, guards, blocks or fights. Recovery doesn't undo a hack, a scam or a transaction you authorised.
6. "Always comes home" is the ritual's promise, not the protocol's. If too few helpers are still reachable, recovery fails. Say that on camera once a season.
7. Helpers are people you choose; the gems are anonymous objects. The human judgement of choosing helpers belongs to the hosts, not to the emblem.
8. The star swap implies that old shares stop working after re-sharing [VERIFY with Facundo against the spec before it is narrated].

## Loop

*Wildcard lane.* A string-figure character: one closed loop of warm gold cord, with five amber beads for hands, feet and heart, that can only stand up when enough of its beads are held.

> Loop is a golden cord that lies in a meaningless squiggle until any three friends' beads land on it, then snaps upright and gets on with its day, so every episode of the show opens with a recovery and closes with a check-in.

### Look

Silhouette: one continuous closed loop of cord with no loose ends. The head is an open ring taking 42% of the figure's height. From the head, the cord runs down and wraps a small ring around the heart bead at the chest, then continues as two curved arms and two curved legs in one unbroken path. Loop is about 2.4 heads tall and stands at 45% of frame height, centred on the page with book-margin negative space. It's a tall figure, so it reframes to 9:16 Shorts without redrawing.

Beads: five identical amber glass beads, two at the hands, two at the feet and one at the heart. Each is 12% of figure height, oversized on purpose because the beads carry legibility at small sizes. They are always round, never cubes.

Face: two eye-dots inside the head ring and no mouth. Blinks, squints and wide eyes, plus posture, carry every emotion.

Cord weight: 5% of figure height. It should read as chunky twisted cord, never a hairline. Limbs always curve, which is the difference between a string person and a stick man.

Palette:
- Cord: core #F4C56A (sunlit gold), highlight #FFE7B0. Printed edge #9C7A36 on paper pages and #B18C44 (the prior package's gold) on ink pages.
- Beads: body #E89A2C, light side #FFD58A, core #A8611A, catch-light #FFFBEF.
- Eyes: ink #123E32 by day, paper #F7F8F1 on night pages.
- Pages: paper #F7F8F1 by day, ink #123E32 by night.
- World linework: jade #1B7466. Flat washes in mint #DCF4E9, sky teal #9ADBD9 and sun #F7D98B. One coral #E8683A prop per episode at most (the orange kayak's colour).
- Supporting cast: the cover's helper robots in #FFFEF8 with #3CC3D6 cyan.

Materials: the cord is two-ply twisted silk with a lit filament core, warm like the light threads in the café still and never a neon tube. The beads are amber glass lit from inside, like the café gems. The world is flat ink on uncoated paper with risograph grain and 1px two-colour misregistration. Every outdoor horizon carries a hairline of the cover's crystalline skyline and one tiny air taxi. That's the touch of modernity inside the printed book.

Rendering: 2D vector at 24 fps. After Effects shape layers on a path rig (or Rive, so Loop can later live on the web and in apps). Loop is animated on ones so it feels crisp and alive; the world is on twos with gentle line boil for the printed-book feel. Loop's own line never boils. The glow is two stacked blurs: additive on night pages, only a faint warm tint on paper.

What reads at 48px: a gold ring over five amber dots. Dots and rings are the last shapes to disappear when an image shrinks. ### Personality

Loop is named after the brief's own line: optimistic about technology because people stay in the loop. It's eager, earnest, helpful and a little bit of a show-off, and never sarcastic. Its whole emotional range is tension: taut means confident and pleased, slack means sheepish or sleepy, a sharp twang means surprise.

The running joke is that Loop improvises props out of itself. Its arm becomes the kite string, the paddle shaft, the harp string. When it gets too ambitious it tangles, sorts itself out with a shrug, and carries on. It's never hurt and never scared.

It's silent, and its voice is plucked string: a rising pluck for "hi", a falling pluck for "whoops", a tremolo for a giggle. So the mascot is literally an instrument the theme can be played on.

It has Bruce's bounce and Facundo's precision. It flops about happily until the moment it snaps exactly into shape. It's rarely alone for long: ensemble episodes pair it with the cover's little white-and-cyan helper robots, the helpful machines doing small kind things.

It never guards, scolds, fights or warns. There are no villains in Loop's world. Its motto, which it never says out loud, is "no loose ends."

### Signature move

THE CHECK-IN: 24 frames, 1.00s, always 3.75s–4.75s.
- f1–6: from whatever pose the activity left it in, Loop glances to camera, hooks a bead-hand into the cord at its chest and gives two small tugs. Its whole body dips twice, with two muted plucks.
- f7–16: a ripple of light runs out along the cord, and the five beads blink warm-white one after another, two frames apart, on a rising five-note kalimba chime.
- f17–24: one satisfied nod-bounce, eyes squinting happily.

This is DeRec's daily check-in, "Still there?" "Still here.", performed as a greeting, so the ritual of the show is the ritual of the protocol and every episode teaches it without a word. It works from any pose: seated in a kayak, mid-air, upside down on a sled. It survives phone size because five dots flashing in sequence is one of the most legible motions there is. The two plucks plus the five-note chime form the show's sonic logo, and the move stands alone as the one-second sting for the weekly short beat.

### How it maps to DeRec, and where the mapping stops

Loop is built on the one picture of secret sharing that is literally true. Shamir's scheme rests on the idea that enough points pin down exactly one curve: two points fix a line, three fix a parabola, and fewer than that fit infinitely many curves. [VERIFY before saying it on air: A. Shamir, "How to Share a Secret", Communications of the ACM, 1979.] Loop is that sentence with eyes. Facundo gets to call it "Lagrange interpolation with eyes"; Grandma gets "you need enough dots to know the shape."

How it maps:
- A single share reveals nothing. With one bead on it, the cord stays a slack squiggle that could become anything. Two beads together (two helpers colluding) still leave it flopping through random shapes: a question mark, a pretzel. The audience never sees half a Loop, because a share is not a piece of the secret.
- A threshold restores. When the third bead lands, the cord snaps taut into the one shape it can be. Beads four and five arrive after the snap because you don't need all of them. Which three beads land first changes every episode, because any three work. That's the Easter egg regulars will eventually notice.
- Helpers don't know about each other. Each bead flies in from a different edge of the frame, held by someone off-screen. Beads never touch and never turn toward each other. Only the cord knows the whole shape, and the cord stands for the owner's app.
- The daily quiet check-in is the signature move. Loop tugs its cord and each bead blinks back: "Still there?" "Still here." The protocol's daily ritual becomes the show's ritual.
- Re-sharing is held back for the episode where it's the topic. A bead drifts away, a new one arrives, and all five beads settle into new positions with a soft re-snap, because a re-share changes every share, not only the new one. [VERIFY that lib-derec generates fresh shares for every helper on re-share.]

Why not the provocations: sourdough, cuttings and seeds give each friend a full copy. That's a backup, the opposite of secret sharing. A jazz trio and a jigsaw both leak, because one part gives away some of the tune or some of the picture. Both would be cute and false.

Where the mapping stops being true:
(1) The cord runs from bead to bead, which suggests helpers are linked to each other. They aren't. We treat the cord as the owner's thread and animate the beads as strangers.
(2) Geometry with ordinary numbers leaks a little: two points on a circle still narrow down where its centre can be. Shamir works in a finite field, where fewer shares than the threshold leak exactly nothing. The picture shows the idea, not the maths.
(3) In the finished pose the beads sit at the hands, feet and heart, so a viewer could read shares as body parts. That's why nothing partial is ever shown.
(4) The snap is instant and theatrical. Real recovery means a new device asking helpers, and the helpers confirming it's really the owner before they release their shares [VERIFY the confirmation step in the current spec]. The real check-in is silent and invisible; ours has a sound.
(5) The picture always shows 3 of 5. DeRec's threshold is roughly half of however many helpers the owner picks, so no numbers ever go on screen.
(6) Loop plays the secret in the snap and the owner's app in the check-in. That's a mascot liberty. Loop is never a helper.
(7) Loop is recovered; it never rescues anything. It doesn't undo hacks, reverse authorised transactions or stop scams, and no bumper implies it does.

Use it on air once: Ep01 hands Bruce his natural first question, "Why does it only stand up on the third bead?", and gives Facundo his cleanest answer.

### Risks

1. The blockchain trap. Beads on a cord can read as "a chain of blocks," the exact crypto cliché we're avoiding and an unfair frame for a chain-agnostic protocol. Rules: beads are round amber glass, never cubes, never evenly spaced in a row, and always at the hands, feet and heart. Never show a necklace. The café still, which the client already liked, shows gems joined by light in a constellation, not a chain; stay on that side.

2. Jigsaw drift. The moment an animator draws "half a Loop" with only two beads, the metaphor becomes false. Rule: below three beads the cord is a random squiggle, never a partial figure. Put it in the animation bible as a hard rule.

3. Stick-man drift. My own proportion test showed that thin, straight limbs and small beads turn Loop into a generic stick figure at thumbnail size. Lock the numbers: head ring 42% of height, beads 12%, cord 5%, curved limbs, a ring around the heart bead. The icon always sits on ink, because pale gold on paper washes out at 48px.

4. Twee. String, beads and paper can slide into craft-fair or preschool TV. Guardrails: one glowing line on a big editorial page, snappy on-ones timing, and the music carrying the modernity. No googly eyes, no blush marks, no mouth, no bows on its head.

5. Too abstract. Cold viewers may not read a line figure as "someone." Test it: show the 48px icon and a five-second cut to five people who've never heard of DeRec. It passes if they say "a little string person." If it fails, thicken the cord and enlarge the beads; do not add a mouth.

6. Overclaim. The one-second snap makes recovery look instant and automatic, and the check-in can make helpers' devices look like people waving. Loop never recovers anything stolen and never saves anyone from a scam or hack. The hosts describe the real flow once, in Ep01 [VERIFY the helper confirmation step]. No numbers on screen, since the threshold is "roughly half," not 3 of 5.

7. A surveillance reading of the check-in. It must feel like a wave between friends: beads blink back warmly and Loop looks glad. Never a scanner beam, a radar sweep or a red status light.

8. Cost. Cheap to run, with some effort to set up. The continuous-loop rig (one closed path that forms limbs without ugly self-crossings) needs a strong 2D rigger for 3–4 days up front. After that, the gather, snap, check-in and handoff are a locked template, and each episode's 2.75s activity is roughly 1–2 animator days; ensemble episodes with robots add about a day. Stop-motion with real gold cord and glass beads on a light table would be gorgeous but costs about three times as much per episode, so save it for the season trailer.

9. AI. None is needed. It's fully hand-animatable, which is a feature on a trust show whose protocol inventor is wary of AI. Any AI-assisted in-betweening or music is a [HOST GATE] decision and must be disclosed.

10. Name. "Loop" is a common word. That's fine for a show mascot, but run a trademark search before any merch [VERIFY].

## Ting

*Wildcard lane, first draft (not judged; its idea survives as the Curve diagram, R6).* A little thread of gold light bent into an arch and strung with three glowing beads. It holds its shape only while enough beads are on it. It is the orbit-thread from the cover, come alive.

> Ting is a little thread of gold light that goes floppy and shapeless when it's missing beads and snaps back into itself, ting!, the moment the third one lands: the real trick inside DeRec, performed by the friendliest squiggle on YouTube.

### Look

SILHOUETTE. A lowercase-n arch standing on two rounded feet, like a croquet hoop with a face. Bounding box 1.1 wide by 1.0 tall. The ribbon's thickness is 18% of its height, uniform, with no taper, rounded end caps and slightly flattened soles. In-world it is about 25 cm tall, somewhere between a coffee mug and a watering can, so it looks small beside the café robot. The ribbon is about 3x its height in length, long enough to become a scarf, a vaulting pole or a hammock.

BEADS. Three identical glass spheres, each 1.6x the ribbon's thickness. They sit at 25%, 50% and 75% along the ribbon, which puts them at the crown and both knees in the resting pose. They keep those proportions in every shape. Never flat discs (coins), never engraved, never stacked.

FACE. Two vertical-oval ink eyes on the front of the crown, just under the crown bead, so the bead reads as the bobble on a hat. No mouth and no voice. All acting is done through posture, squash and blinks.

PALETTE.
- Ribbon: honey gold #E9B949, shade #B8862B, highlight #FFE08A. This is matched to the gold of "DeRec" in the cover wordmark so the character and the wordmark belong together. [VERIFY] against the brand master.
- Beads: core #FFF4D6, amber rim #F0A93B, bloom #FFD36E at 40%. They echo the amber gems in 04-cafe-constellation.
- Eyes and keyline: ink #123E32.
- World, from the prior package: paper #F7F8F1, mint #DCF4E9, jade #1B7466. Added: sky #BFE3F0, terracotta #D9774B for autumn and the orange kayak, and cyan #5CCFE6 used only on the café robot.
- Night plate: #18264A. At night the keyline drops out and Ting glows.

MATERIALS. Satin, like silk embroidery floss lit from inside. Its physical twin is a bent brass rod with three amber glass beads, which can sit on the table between the hosts. It embroiders cleanly as a jacket patch next to the shield.

RENDERING. 2D vector with a ligne-claire ink keyline and flat colour. The only effects are one soft bloom on the beads and a single glint on the snap. Backgrounds are flat "printed plate" paintings of the solarpunk city with light risograph grain, three depth planes at most, so they read on a phone. Ting is animated on twos for hand-made warmth; the snap and glint are on ones for modern crispness. So "classic structure, modern light" is built into the frame rate. Why 2D and not glossy 3D: it hits the brief's printed-book target, keeps the character fully drawable by hand, and holds perfectly consistent across 12 episodes.

WHY IT READS AT 48px. A gold arch, three bright points and two dark eyes. The beads survive downscaling because they are the brightest pixels in the frame, and the ink keyline keeps gold legible against cream. The near-square arch fills a round YouTube avatar. Test this on a phone before anything else is drawn.

### Personality

A silent physical comedian with Bruce's delight and Facundo's timing. Ting throws itself sincerely into whatever it is doing, wobbles, recovers, and is quietly proud when it lands it. It is curious, game for anything and a bit of a show-off. It is kind in small ways: it arches over a robot in the rain without being asked. It is at ease rather than reckless. It knows its friends have it, but it still looks both ways. The comedy is deadpan: one small thing goes right or wrong, and it is never a pratfall that hurts. Ting is named for the sound it makes when it comes together, the last note of the theme, which regulars will end up humming. The name also carries two happy accidents: in Mandarin, 听 (tīng) means "listen", which suits a podcast, and in Old Norse the thing/ting was the community assembly, which suits an alliance. House rules: Ting never speaks (its only sounds are the plink, the slack "bwoing" and the ting). It never breaks, gets cut or tangles. It never guards, fights or locks anything, and it never winks at money or crypto. In the bumper it never shares a shot with the hosts. It can appear with them in the topic segment and as the brass desk object.

### Signature move

THE TING. It lasts 24 frames (1.00s at 24 fps), is identical every episode and is reused frame for frame.
- f1–2 (0.00s–0.08s): Ting hops. The anticipation squash happens at the end of the activity beat.
- f3–6 (0.08s–0.25s): at the top of the hop the three beads pop off like popcorn and the thread flops into a scribble. These four frames say "could be anything". They are the only frames allowed to change between episodes: the scribble secretly sketches the next episode's activity, a reward for regulars who pause.
- f7 (0.25s): bead one drops back on. Plink.
- f10 (0.375s): bead two drops back on. Plink. The thread is still wobbling, because two beads aren't enough.
- f13 (0.50s): bead three lands. The thread snaps taut into the arch with a bright bell TING.
- f13–18 (0.50s–0.75s): a glint runs along the thread from crown to feet.
- f19–24 (0.75s–1.00s): Ting lands in its arch with a small squash and blinks once at camera.
Plink, plink, TING is the show's sonic logo and the last three notes of the theme: two 16th-note pickups into a downbeat at 120 bpm. The whole move is threshold recovery in one second. It still reads with the sound off, because the change from slack to taut is visible.

### How it maps to DeRec, and where the mapping stops

WHY A THREAD AND BEADS. Shamir's Secret Sharing, the engine of DeRec, hides the secret on a curve and gives each helper one point on that curve. Ting is the curve and its beads are the points. Every cryptography lecturer draws this picture, so Facundo can defend it line by line. Ting is the SECRET, not a helper or the owner. The show's mascot is the thing worth protecting, and it is cheerful, not anxious. The brief's provocations fail this test. Every sourdough jar and every cutting is the whole thing, which makes it a backup, the opposite of a share. A jigsaw piece shows part of the picture. A bead shows nothing.

WHAT IS TRUE.
- One share reveals nothing. With one or two beads the thread flops through every shape at once, because endless curves pass through two points. Below the threshold, every possible secret is equally likely. The flop is Ting's running gag, and it is the real security property, not decoration.
- A threshold restores. The third bead leaves exactly one curve that fits: snap, and Ting is Ting. Ting is a three-bead curve, which in maths is a parabola. In Ting's world, five beads live in five windows across the city and any three will do: 3-of-5, roughly half, as in DeRec. [VERIFY] DeRec's default threshold with Facundo before saying numbers on camera.
- Helpers don't know each other. This is a staging law. Beads travel in from different directions. They never share a frame until they are on Ting, and they never greet each other. The windows never face one another. Only the owner, who is never shown, knows where all five are.
- The owner quietly checks in every day. In explainers and in the weekly short, Ting sends a soft pulse to each window and each window blinks back once. Nobody else sees it. That is the weekly beat's whole bumper: five blinks, one ting.
- Re-sharing. When Ting changes (a new shape is a new secret) or a window goes dark, every bead is replaced by a fresh one in a new place, and the old beads fit nothing. [VERIFY] with Facundo that a DeRec re-share sends fresh shares to every helper and makes the old shares useless.

WHERE IT STOPS BEING TRUE (say it before a commenter does).
1. In Shamir the secret is one number, the point where the curve crosses zero, not the whole curve. Ting-as-the-secret is a simplification.
2. Real shares are points in modular arithmetic, so there is no smooth line to look at. The smooth curve is the classroom picture, not the cryptography.
3. The flop gag exaggerates. Through two points, a three-bead curve only wobbles between parabolas. It never becomes a duck or a teapot.
4. The bumper shows a recovery every episode. Real life is the opposite: beads stay home for years and just blink back. Treat the bumper as a fire drill, not a daily event.
5. Beads never fly on their own initiative. Helpers hand back shares only in a recovery the owner starts. [VERIFY] the helper-side confirmation step. The pop-and-drop in the signature is a hop, not a protocol message.
6. Ting coming back together is not protection from everything. Recovery doesn't undo a hack, reverse a payment or stop a scam. Ting never fights, guards or survives an attack on screen.

BONUS. Because Ting is built from the real mechanism, it doubles as the diagram in the topic segment. One bead on screen with the thread flopping answers "what can one helper see?" in two seconds, and it passes the Grandma test.

### Risks

TWEE. A glowing thread with feelings can turn greeting-card very fast. Guard: no mouth, no voice, no sparkle trails apart from the one glint, and deadpan physical comedy. The flop is always funny-ugly, never pretty.

MISREADS. At a glance Ting could read as a noodle, a worm, a horseshoe magnet or the letter n. Guard: both ends identical and no taper or segments (so it can't be a worm), all three beads visible in every frame, and the 48px phone test done first.

OVERCLAIM. "Ting always comes back together" can slide into "your secret is always safe". Never caption Ting with safety language. Never show it surviving a hacker, a lock or a storm played as a threat. Never let "brave because recoverable" become "careless because recoverable". Recovery is not a cure-all, and the mascot must not imply one.

CRYPTO-CLICHÉ DRIFT. Gold beads could drift toward coins or tokens. Beads are glass spheres, never discs, never stamped, never stacked. No chain-link imagery: Ting is a thread, not a chain.

TOO CLEVER. The maths is a gift for Facundo and the engineers, never a requirement. The bumper explains nothing. Grandma just sees a gold squiggle that goes ting.

NAME. "Ting" needs a trademark check and a quick native-speaker check in the Alliance's main languages [VERIFY]. Ting is an existing US internet brand. These names were rejected on purpose:
- Tether: a major stablecoin.
- Curve and Arc: both are crypto protocols.
- Dash: a coin.
- Polly: Polygon, Polymarket, and the 2021 Poly Network hack.
- Ada: Cardano's token. Cardano is an Alliance member, so never.

MUTED AUTOPLAY. The sonic logo is half the ritual. The snap from slack to taut has to carry it silently, and it only will if the flop really flops.

LINEAGE. Single-line characters have ancestors: Cavandoli's La Linea, and Barbapapa's shape-shifting. Ting is an object with beads, never a line that draws its own world, and no animator's hand ever appears.

COST. Low. The rig is one ribbon spine, three beads and two eyes, about a day to build in vector. The signature is animated once and reused. Per bumper, roughly 2–3 animator-days including the background plate [VERIFY with the studio]. The shape-gag episodes (pole vault, hammock, scarf) cost the most, because the spine has to deform. Generative video is a poor fit, since bead counts and eye positions drift from shot to shot. That conveniently points production at the hand-animated route, which a wary inventor will prefer. Any AI use, including for background plates, is a [HOST GATE] decision and must be disclosed.
